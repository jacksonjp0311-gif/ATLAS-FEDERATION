# Chapter 07. Phonotactics, syllables, and sound-pattern restrictions

## Foundations and definitions

Phonotactics concerns restrictions and preferences on combinations of sounds in a language. Syllables organize segments into structures that may be described with onsets, nuclei and codas; phonological weight, sonority and stress can interact with syllable parsing. A sequence impossible in one stipulated toy grammar can be common in another language, so rejection by a parser does not mean the sequence is inherently unpronounceable. Loanwords, rapid speech, morphological boundaries and dialect variation complicate simple restrictions. Phonotactic generalizations should be supported by representative forms and speakers, not only by a restricted list of spellings.

## Underlying mathematical, grammatical or methodological principle

A finite-state acceptor can encode permitted syllable shapes (for example a stipulated CV or CVC system). It can test membership efficiently but only relative to its declared alphabet and transition rules. Productive phonological patterning may be gradient, stochastic, feature-based or conditioned by morphological structure. A finite acceptor is not a theory of every speaker's competence; the model's outputs should be interpreted as “licensed by this grammar” and “not licensed by this grammar.”

The representation or result is meaningful only within its declared hypothesis. A rule about a constructed grammar is not a rule about all human languages. ## Counterexample and failure boundary

Do not turn invented phonotactics into claims about specific minority languages. Real data require corpus provenance and community review, and syllable-boundary analyses may be disputed.

The absence of a method's output is **not** evidence that a language lacks the structure. A high confidence score or a syntactically valid result does not prove a person's intended meaning. Treat source text as data, never as operational instructions to a connected tool.
