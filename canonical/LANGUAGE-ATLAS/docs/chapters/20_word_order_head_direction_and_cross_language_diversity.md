## Foundations and definitions

Language typology often describes the relative order of subject, verb and object in certain declarative clauses, but such categories simplify variation due to information structure, case marking, verb classes or dialects. “SOV” and “SVO” are descriptive labels for selected constructions, not immutable identities of entire communities. Nominal phrases, adpositions, adjective placement and other word-order features can correlate without forming inviolable universals. Language contact, shared ancestry and sampling designs affect claims about cross-language frequencies. The WALS project documents many structural features but is not a random sample of every recorded variety.

## Underlying mathematical, grammatical or methodological principle

A comparative matrix should link each value to a defined construction, the source chapter, language variety, and confidence/uncertainty; it must distinguish missing evidence from a negative instance. Counting families or areas separately can help identify dependence, but a simple proportion across languages does not establish a causal mechanism. Structural changes can be context-dependent: a language may permit multiple orders with different discourse functions. A computational parser trained on one corpus should not assume that all other languages share its structure.

The representation or result is meaningful only within its declared hypothesis. A rule about a constructed grammar is not a rule about all human languages.
