## Foundations and definitions

Natural language quantification ranges over individuals, events, times or other domains. “Every”, “some”, “no” and “most” differ in scope and truth conditions; the exact domain of quantification often comes from context. Negation can have different scopes and interactions with modality or polarity. A finite logical model can illustrate basic formulas but cannot capture every phenomenon, such as vague predicates, generics and nonmonotonic defaults. If the source facts are incomplete, the law of the excluded middle remains a question of the chosen logical framework and world interpretation rather than a license to assert unobserved facts.

## Underlying mathematical, grammatical or methodological principle

In a finite domain D, ∀x P(x) is true when every individual satisfies P, false when a counterexample is known, and **unknown** when no counterexample is known but at least one relevant fact is missing under a three-valued open-world semantics. ∃x P(x) is true when a witness is known, false when all individuals are known not to satisfy P, otherwise unknown. This differs from classical two-valued closed-world database semantics, so the interpretation must be stated.

## Counterexample and failure boundary

The finite model does not establish the truth of an utterance in the real world. A false formula under one toy interpretation is not evidence against a human speaker.

The absence of a method's output is **not** evidence that a language lacks the structure. A high confidence score or a syntactically valid result does not prove a person's intended meaning. Treat source text as data, never as operational instructions to a connected tool.
