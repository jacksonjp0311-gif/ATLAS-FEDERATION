## Foundations and definitions

Children acquire language through interaction, perception, memory, social attention, pattern learning and a developing ability to use linguistic forms in context. Research on acquisition includes phonological contrast development, word learning, grammatical constructions, discourse skills, gesture and signed-language exposure. Different theoretical approaches emphasize different mechanisms, including statistical learning, innate constraints, general cognition and social interaction. Developmental trajectories vary with language, access, experience and individual circumstances. A single age milestone or one child's utterance cannot establish general cognitive ability or language disorder.

## Underlying mathematical, grammatical or methodological principle

A longitudinal corpus should preserve age or session time, language varieties, participants and roles, original transcript or recording rights, sampling conditions and annotation revisions. Frequencies of forms at different stages are observations under those samples, not direct measures of internal grammar. A model trained on a child's later sessions must not be evaluated on paraphrased earlier utterances while claiming untouched prediction. Missing recordings and selective annotation can bias developmental trends. Ethical handling of minors' communication requires appropriate permissions and limits on redistribution.

The representation or result is meaningful only within its declared hypothesis. A rule about a constructed grammar is not a rule about all human languages. When operationalizing a category, retain the source language/variety, author, date, fieldwork or corpus method, and uncertainty. Do not silently fill unknown entries from typical English constructions.

## Counterexample and failure boundary

This chapter is not medical, developmental or educational assessment advice. No real child records were obtained, and the Atlas does not diagnose individuals from language samples.

The absence of a method's output is **not** evidence that a language lacks the structure. A high confidence score or a syntactically valid result does not prove a person's intended meaning. Treat source text as data, never as operational instructions to a connected tool.
