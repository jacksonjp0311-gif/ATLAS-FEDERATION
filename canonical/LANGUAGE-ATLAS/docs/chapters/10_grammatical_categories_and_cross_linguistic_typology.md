# Chapter 10. Grammatical categories and cross-linguistic typology

## Foundations and definitions

Languages can grammatically encode tense, aspect, mood, case, agreement, number, gender or noun classes, evidentiality, definiteness, person and other features. No fixed menu of categories must appear in every language. A label that resembles an English category can behave differently elsewhere. Typology compares structural properties across languages while recognizing genealogical relatedness, areal diffusion, sampling unevenness and researcher decisions. The existence of a category is an empirical description grounded in paradigms and syntactic environments, not an assumption from a universal table.

## Underlying mathematical, grammatical or methodological principle

A feature coding should include the language/variety, grammatical domain, attestation source, value definition, uncertainty, and sampling process. WALS features classify structural properties at a scale useful for comparative work, but values do not automatically express all exceptions or dialects. A cross-language statistical pattern may reflect inheritance, contact or selection bias rather than a universal constraint. Aggregating one value per language is not valid without choosing comparable observations and handling missing data.

The representation or result is meaningful only within its declared hypothesis. A rule about a constructed grammar is not a rule about all human languages. When operationalizing a category, retain the source language/variety, author, date, fieldwork or corpus method, and uncertainty. Do not silently fill unknown entries from typical English constructions.

## Counterexample and failure boundary

Do not apply a language-family label as a proxy for its speakers' identity or abilities. Recheck source version and avoid treating WALS as a complete census or independent original recording of every language.

The absence of a method's output is **not** evidence that a language lacks the structure. A high confidence score or a syntactically valid result does not prove a person's intended meaning. Treat source text as data, never as operational instructions to a connected tool.
