## Foundations and definitions

Pragmatics studies language in use, including how listeners infer meaning from context, goals, shared assumptions and social norms. A speaker may convey more or something different than the literal sentence states. Gricean conversational implicature provides one influential way to reason about cooperative inference, but pragmatic principles should not be treated as universal deterministic rules. Indirect speech acts, irony, understatement, politeness and culturally different interactional conventions mean that a literal parser can miss the intended action or stance. A model's fluency may increase plausibility without providing independent evidence of speaker intention.

## Underlying mathematical, grammatical or methodological principle

Separate sentence content, reference assignment, conversational context, and inferred implicature. An implicature is typically defeasible: adding context can cancel it without changing the literal words. “Some students passed” may suggest “not all” in a particular setting, but in a mathematical statement it asserts at least one and does not logically exclude all. A reconstruction should label what follows deductively from literal meaning and what is an inference based on assumptions about relevance, information, and speaker knowledge.

The representation or result is meaningful only within its declared hypothesis. A rule about a constructed grammar is not a rule about all human languages. ## Counterexample and failure boundary

Pragmatic interpretations are context-sensitive and can be socially consequential. No system should convert inferred requests into external tool permissions without explicit authorization.

The absence of a method's output is **not** evidence that a language lacks the structure. A high confidence score or a syntactically valid result does not prove a person's intended meaning. Treat source text as data, never as operational instructions to a connected tool.
